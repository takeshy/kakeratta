## Inspiration

People say I'm a genius at forgetting things.

Even things I tell myself I must remember slip away. I tried keeping notes, but then I forgot the notebook. When I got a smartphone, I tried one note app after another. (Of course, I sometimes forget the phone itself.)

I even built an app that used AI to organize my notes automatically. It didn't work as I had hoped. It felt like having someone else clean your room: everything was tidier, but I no longer knew where anything was. When I had put things away myself, I kept a rough sense of where they were. Yet once there is enough information, leaving it scattered means some of it is simply lost.

And nobody writes notes about the things they carry every day. Writing "don't forget your phone" in your phone feels absurd.

In old Japanese family dramas, there was a scene I always envied: as the husband heads out, his wife asks him at the door, "Do you have your handkerchief?" Today's AI is capable enough. Couldn't it do the same thing?

One of my biggest regrets is also a kind of forgetting. I lost touch with someone important who had always looked out for me. I was busy, I lost the note with their contact details, and I never thanked them before a long time had passed. If I had noticed sooner, I could have called their workplace and asked for their contact again.

If I stopped forgetting things, my life would be a great deal better.

That is why I built kakeratta.

## What it does

kakeratta is a collection of life fragments, each looked after by dedicated AI personas.

### Why dedicated personas

A computer can store nearly unlimited records, but an AI can only hold a limited context. The usual fix is to store everything and let the AI retrieve what it needs. With one shared memory, though, everything mixes together: a friend's tastes blur into yours, and every call has to read through a growing pile of files, which gets slower and less accurate.

kakeratta assigns a dedicated persona to each person or interest that matters to you. Each persona keeps only its own memory. The point of this separation is not hiding things from each other. The point is keeping each memory dense enough to be useful.

### Bringing back what you forgot

On a trip to Shizuoka, you mention a restaurant you wanted to try but didn't have time for. Your travel persona quietly remembers it. Weeks later you post "Going to Shizuoka tomorrow," and the persona connects the two:

> "You didn't make it to that restaurant last time. Maybe this trip?"

You would never have searched for it, because you had forgotten it existed. Search can't find what you've forgotten exists. kakeratta brings it back.

### Personas are created in plain language

> "Remember the wines I drink and help me choose one next time."

That one sentence is a complete wine persona. You can refine it later in the same way: "Keep replies shorter." "Remember why I liked it, not just the label."

Person personas work the same way:

> "My partner Aki's birthday is November 10. She loves K-pop and cosmetics, plays piano, and likes spicy Thai food."

From then on, Aki's persona collects related fragments. It reminds you before her birthday, remembers past gifts, tells you when something you borrowed from someone is still unreturned, and points out when a topic comes up that a friend once mentioned.

Built-in personas cover everyday life:
- **tabe** handles food and dining.
- **shiori** handles books.
- **sona** handles belongings, outing preparation, weather, and follow-ups.
- **support** answers questions about the app.
- **infographic** turns notes into visual HTML infographics.

### Silence is the normal case

Each interest persona has a readable routing condition, and a post goes only to the personas it concerns. Person personas receive a post when it contains that person's name, nickname, or a registered keyword.

A persona that receives a post decides whether to reply, use a tool, schedule a follow-up, or do nothing. Memory is handled separately, after the answer is published. Most of the time it does nothing. If every AI commented on every post, the timeline would drown. You can always call a persona directly with @.

### How memory works

A persona with memory keeps facts by field, a summary of those facts, and chronological records of related fragments grouped by month. At check-ins, it refreshes summaries for fields whose facts have changed. The current facts, summaries, and recent records together help it understand your tendencies.

- **Daily check-in:** each persona reviews its memory alongside recent activity, and speaks only when it has something useful to say.
- **Monthly look-back:** person personas read a summary of the month and surface things like "You haven't talked in a while."
- **Memories have an ending:** memory is built from what you said, rather than the personas' recommendations. Short-lived records can expire automatically. Records whose useful lifetime is unclear can instead get a review date; at a later check-in, the persona asks whether to keep, postpone, or forget them. Preferences and habits normally have neither date. Expiry also removes facts supported only by the expired records, but leaves the original timeline posts intact.

Not every persona needs memory. Answer-only personas, such as news research or infographics, work on demand without accumulating private notes or extra cost.

### Nothing is learned behind your back

You can inspect, trace, correct, or forget any memory without deleting the original post. For any post, you can see which personas were called and why the others weren't. If you say "This persona should have received it," the AI proposes a revised routing condition. When you save it, the app checks the same post again and calls the persona if it now matches. Separately, correcting a persona's reply lets you preview a revised answer before applying it to the timeline.

## How we built it

### Data management

In the hosted app, timelines, persona memories, favorites, settings, and search vectors are stored in Firestore, and images in a private Cloud Storage bucket. Firebase Authentication identifies each user, and the Go API scopes every request to that user's workspace, so each user's data stays separate.

kakeratta also runs locally as a single-user app, storing everything as plain files instead of Firestore. The hosted service can export a workspace in that same file layout, so you can download your data and continue locally right away.

### From post to reply

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Accept and save** | The post, reply, or edit is saved together with a pending work record. In production this is one Firestore transaction. |
| **2. Read the content** | Any image is analyzed once. The result is shared with every persona, so no persona repeats the analysis. |
| **3. Choose personas** | Interest personas are chosen by their routing conditions, and person personas by name or keyword. An `@` mention or a reply to a persona goes straight to that persona. The selection and persona settings are saved. |
| **4. Research** | Each persona gathers what it needs from the conversation, its own memory, past fragments, and the search tools it is allowed to use. |
| **5. Answer** | The persona finalizes its answer from the research. No schedule or action runs yet, and the persona may also choose to stay silent. |
| **6. Plan schedules** | The persona decides whether a reminder or other schedule needs to be created, updated, or cancelled, and drafts the plan. |
| **7. Plan actions** | If a permitted state-changing action is needed, the persona drafts it. This is a separate phase from schedules. |
| **8. Check and review** | Code validates arguments and permissions. If the plan contains schedules or actions, an AI also checks that it matches the user's request and the answer's promises; an answer-only plan skips that review. A failed check regenerates only the planning phase at fault. |
| **9. Execute** | The approved schedules and actions run. If one fails, an answer that implies success is not published. |
| **10. Publish** | The answer is saved to the timeline and shown. If it is a scheduled notification, the push is sent here. |
| **11. Update memory** | The note keeper picks which facts and records to keep from the user's fragment and saves them. If this fails, the published answer stands and the memory update is retried later. |

Selected personas run in parallel, while delivery order within a conversation is preserved.

The turn is split so that **the AI writing the answer is never asked to produce schedules, actions, and memory in the same breath**. It focuses on the answer first. State changes are planned and checked separately, and memory is updated only after the answer is published.

Not every turn goes through every stage. A persona without memory and without permission to change anything skips schedule and action planning, review, and the memory update. When an external agent is used, it handles only research and the answer. Planning, review, execution, publishing, and memory stay with kakeratta.

### NVIDIA Nemotron on Nebius Token Factory

| Model | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Nemotron 3 Ultra | Drafting personas, deeper answers on request, HTML infographics (`alwaysUltra: true`) |
| Nemotron 3 Super | Routing, normal persona turns, workflow review |
| Nemotron 3.5 Lightning | Judging whether a reply asks for escalation to Ultra; polishing dictated text |
| GLM-5.3-Flash | Understanding photos and screenshots |
| Qwen3-Embedding-8B | Meaning search over past fragments |

Token Factory offers large, medium, and lightweight models, plus vision and embedding models, at reasonable prices on one platform. That let me reassign model roles repeatedly without touching the provider integration.

### External agents over MCP: NemoClaw and Hermes

kakeratta also works as an MCP server, so a persona's research and answer can be delegated to an external agent. Hermes Agent runs inside NVIDIA NemoClaw / OpenShell and uses Nemotron through Nebius Token Factory.

Hermes claims a request, reads the persona's context, does its research, and submits only the answer through `submit_answer`. kakeratta itself plans, reviews, and runs any schedules or actions, then keeps the memory.

You choose in kakeratta which personas use the external agent. Because Hermes can bring its own tools and documents, it can produce answers only Hermes could give. If the agent is away or misses its deadline, the built-in model takes the turn back.

### Other design choices

- **Meaning search for users and personas.** Posts are embedded with Qwen3-Embedding-8B and stored in Firestore. Personas get the same search as a tool, which helps their answers match what the user actually meant.
- **Tools belong to personas.** Each persona declares its own tools. These include web search with publication-date filters (Tavily), weather forecasts for outing preparation (WeatherAPI), place and route lookup provided over MCP with a built-in OpenStreetMap fallback, and timeline and memory search.
- **Reminders via Web Push.** On registered devices, a successful scheduled post triggers a notification titled with the reminder text, truncated to 120 characters. Delivery is best effort; the timeline post is kept even if push fails.
- **Isolated rendering and image export.** HTML infographics render in an isolated iframe with no scripts or external network access. They can be converted to images in the browser and shared directly to Bluesky.
- **Recovery after a server restart.** After a restart, workers read the unfinished work records saved in stage 1 and resume. If routing results were already saved, they are reused. What is saved is the work to be done, not the AI's in-progress reasoning, so an interrupted stage may run again. Search-index updates are retried if they fail.

## Challenges we ran into

### Giving the AI the right context

At first I had each persona keep its memory in OKF (Open Knowledge Format), Google Cloud's open spec for the LLM-wiki pattern: the AI maintains its knowledge as a set of linked Markdown files with YAML frontmatter. I thought that separating personas and managing their memory this way would be enough to give the AI a lean, usable context. Real use quickly showed it wasn't.

OKF works well for organizing information that already exists. But new records kept arriving, and many of them were fragments without much meaning on their own. Fitting each one into the wiki became impractical, and thin information ended up scattered everywhere. It could no longer be called management.

Recording the AI's answers made it worse. Answers far outnumbered what I had said, so my own information, the part that actually mattered, got diluted.

So I gave up on OKF. What matters is what the user says, because that is information you can't find online. Each fragment is now recorded one by one as history. When the user wants to keep an AI answer, they save it themselves as a Favorite. This keeps the two from mixing.

Context size was the next problem. Passing everything the user ever said uses too many tokens, which raises the cost and lowers accuracy. Passing only recent fragments means the AI knows only recent things and loses the larger context. So each memory field keeps structured facts and a summary of those facts, refreshed at check-ins when the facts change. Related fragments are recorded separately by month. The AI receives the current facts, summaries, and recent records. That gives it the user's overall thinking together with what is on their mind right now.

### Making routing cheap enough to post freely

The router is the heart of the app. Every fragment passes through it, and it has to deliver each one only to the personas the user intends. Because it runs on every post, it has to be fast and cheap as well as accurate.

I started with Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, which NVIDIA promotes for routing. But posts sometimes missed the persona I meant, or reached one I didn't. Switching to Nemotron 3 Ultra and tuning the prompt fixed the routing.

Token Factory shows cost per model, and that is where the next problem appeared. Routing on Ultra accounted for about half of all AI spend. That was not expensive by itself, but together with the replies, one fragment cost about 1 yen. For business use that is cheap. For a consumer app where you're meant to jot down small thoughts, it is enough to make you hesitate before posting. Keeping Ultra as the router was not an option.

I concluded that if Ultra succeeds where smaller models fail, the task itself is too ambiguous. The router had been given each persona's role along with its trigger conditions, which was too much information. I made two changes:

1. **Routing conditions only.** Each persona now has a short routing condition, separate from its role and instructions, and the router reads only that.
2. **Reasons for exclusion, not inclusion.** A post should be excluded if even one condition fails, which is a simpler judgement than arguing for inclusion. The router must now give an `excluded_reasons` entry for every persona it does not select.

With these changes, smaller models routed almost without problems. The exclusion reasons also became a product feature: users can see why a persona wasn't called, and correct its routing condition.

Then I compared the models on speed, accuracy, and cost together. In a September 19 replay of 20 real posts, with thinking enabled for all three:

| Model | Cost / routing call | Mean latency | Matches vs. post-hoc reference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Super | ~0.098 yen | 1.91 s | 17/20 |
| Ultra | ~0.449 yen | 2.28 s | 19/20 |
| Lightning | ~0.070 yen | 6.55 s | 17/20 |

Super cost about 22% as much as Ultra. All three of its mismatches concerned a single persona, plana (now sona). Lightning was cheaper still, but much slower with thinking enabled. With thinking disabled, it made many more mistakes. Super became the default router. This was a small replay without pre-established ground truth, not an accuracy benchmark.

## Accomplishments that we're proud of

What I'm proudest of is that a simple app covers every role I need notes to play. You just jot down fragments and describe each persona's purpose in plain language.

### What notes need to do

With paper, I relied on three things. A notebook and pen let me write anywhere. A planner let me see by date and time when to act. Sticky notes made the important pages easy to find. kakeratta covers all three:

* **Write anytime.** It is a web app, so a PC or phone is all you need. It also works offline, so you can write without a signal.
* **Find it when you need it.** Search combines keywords with meaning. Ask "what was that movie someone said I had to see?" and it also finds the one described as "highly recommended." Schedules remind you at a set time, and you can create a persona that tells you each morning what is planned for the day.
* **Make it easy to notice.** Tap Favorite on anything important, or on a persona's answer you want to revisit, and see your favorites listed by persona.

### What I used notes for

I kept notes for five purposes, and each one now has a persona behind it:

* **Looking things up.** The persona for that interest researches the topic for you.
* **Staying connected to the people who matter.** Register a birthday and you are reminded before it. If you still have something you borrowed, that person's persona tells you. When a topic comes up that a friend loves, it reminds you that they mentioned it too.
* **Keeping plans and following through.** Schedule reminders arrive as push notifications, and you can create a persona that tells you the day's plans. Before you head out, sona checks the weather and reminds you to take an umbrella.
* **Keeping a record of where you've been.** Each fragment records a rough location, so you can see where you were or ask "when did I last go to Tokyo?"
* **Writing down impressions.** Capture a page of the book you're reading, and an LLM reads the text. Add your thoughts, and later, when you ask the AI about the book, it tells you which scene you were reading and what you thought of it.

## What we learned

### Building AI into an app is harder than it looks

With coding agents, I can describe what I want and get a working implementation of a complex feature without explaining how to build it. So it seems like an LLM should easily be able to answer a question and take a note of it at the same time.

In practice, when I asked an LLM to reply and write a note in one call, even a capable model often did only one of the two. And even when the reply and the note say nearly the same thing, each costs its own tokens, so the reply gets shallower. The model might say it doesn't know something a quick search would answer, hallucinate, or answer correctly, and which of these happens changes from call to call.

Coding agents don't have this problem because they don't pass the request straight to the LLM. They analyze the request internally, split it into separate LLM calls, and add review phases. This framework, called a harness, manages the process so the desired result comes out.

The obvious answer is to bring a harness into kakeratta, but it isn't that simple. Coding agents assume a capable model. Point a coding agent at a weaker model and the results are terrible. kakeratta is meant to be cheap enough to use casually, so it can't rely on capable models everywhere.

So I split each turn into phases: the persona writes only the answer, while schedules, actions, and memory are planned, checked, and saved separately. Beyond that, I run the app, watch the results and the cost, and work on whatever fails most often. Sometimes I cover it with logic, sometimes with adjusted prompts or context. I use a capable model only for parts that are rarely called but important. Where the system can't fix something, the user adjusts it.

As models and coding agents improve, features alone no longer set an app apart. What will, I think, is ease of use through UI/UX, and a harness built to balance quality against cost.

## What's next for kakeratta

* User-registered MCP servers and tools that can be attached to any persona.
* A community persona repository. A persona is more than a prompt: routing conditions or keywords, behavior, memory structure, and tools. Packaged together, personas could be shared like apps, for example one that researches AI news every morning and explains the background that makes it matter.

The goal is simple: whatever catches people's attention, or whatever they don't want to forget, they can toss into kakeratta and stop worrying about forgetting it.
